\subsection{RQ1: The Reference Forecast Changes Both Magnitude and Shape}
\label{sec:rq1-reference}

Holding the model, the data and the training procedure fixed, we
compute skill for the same forecasts against two references: smart
persistence and the convex reference. The convex reference's weight
$w$ is fitted on the validation split (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}), so
validation skill against it is in-sample for the reference; we report
the held-out test year first, since it is the clean measurement.

On the test split (2015), evaluated once after every modelling and
evaluation choice was frozen, skill against persistence for gradient
boosting on array 11 is 0.266, 0.574 and 0.701 at one, three and six
hours; against the convex reference, over the same forecasts, it is
0.210, 0.316 and 0.232. The pattern holds on all three arrays: 0.263
to 0.202, 0.556 to 0.305 and 0.684 to 0.225 on array 12; 0.077 to
0.043, 0.281 to 0.156 and 0.381 to 0.144 on array 17.

The shape, not only the magnitude, changes, and the test split
establishes this cleanly, out-of-sample for the reference. Measured
against persistence, skill rises monotonically with horizon on every
array - the pattern commonly reported as evidence that a method's
advantage grows at longer lead times. Measured against the convex
reference, skill is non-monotonic on every array, peaking at three
hours and falling back by six, on data no evaluation choice was tuned
against.

Fig.~\ref{fig:F1} illustrates the same shape on the validation split
(2014), the split on which $w$ was fit and therefore in-sample for the
reference: the solid curve (persistence) rises monotonically in every
panel; the dashed curve (convex reference) peaks at three hours in
every panel and falls back at six. On validation, skill against
persistence for gradient boosting on array 11 is 0.252, 0.527 and
0.652; against the convex reference it is 0.200, 0.276 and 0.194
(Table~\ref{tab:T2}). At six hours, 70 percent of the apparent skill
relative to persistence disappears when the same forecasts are
evaluated against the convex reference - an upper bound, since $w$ was
fit to minimise error on this exact split. The pattern holds on all
three arrays: 0.244 to 0.190, 0.510 to 0.275 and 0.639 to 0.201 on
array 12; 0.166 to 0.122, 0.341 to 0.183 and 0.386 to 0.132 on array
17.

The reference effect is not a normalisation artifact. Table~\ref{tab:T3}
gives the absolute RMSE behind the validation skill numbers above:
persistence itself deteriorates sharply with horizon, from 0.428 kW at
one hour to 1.548 kW at six, while climatology remains flat at 0.669
kW throughout. The denominator changes because the baseline genuinely
changes in quality, not because of how the ratio is formed.

\input{tables/T3_rmse.tex}

The mechanism is structural. Smart persistence forecasts $\hat{P}(t) =
k_p(t-h) \times P_{cs}(t)$, and at a six-hour horizon a midday target
is issued near dawn, when no valid clear-sky index yet exists: the
proportion of daylight forecasts relying on a forward-filled rather
than directly observed index rises from 3.3 percent at one hour to
22.6 percent at three and 51 percent at six, and the resulting bias
reaches $-2.15$ kW at midday on a 5 kW array. The fitted convex weight
$w$ reflects this directly, falling from 0.77 at one hour to 0.25 at
three and 0.04 at six on array 11 - at long horizons the optimal
reference is almost entirely climatology, which has no comparable
staleness problem. Array 17 is a partial exception: its fitted weight
remains higher at every horizon (0.83, 0.50, 0.31), because its
persistence forecast is stronger in relative terms, and its
non-monotonic peak in Fig.~\ref{fig:F1} is correspondingly shallower.

Under a paired Diebold-Mariano test with HAC (Bartlett-kernel)
variance and the Harvey-Leybourne-Newbold small-sample correction
\cite{diebold1995comparing, harvey1997testing}, the convex reference is
supported by Holm-adjusted Diebold-Mariano tests on the held-out test
split, beating smart persistence in all nine array-horizon cells
($p_{\text{holm}} < 0.05$); the corresponding validation-split
comparisons, reported descriptively because $w$ is fitted to minimise
error on that exact split, are significant in all nine cells there as
well. The reference recommended by Yang et al. is not merely a
construction, it is a significantly harder target.

Array 17's absolute skill shifts between years by more than the other
two arrays' does, and the shift traces to its reference rather than
its models: array 17's convex weight leans more heavily on
persistence at every horizon (0.31 to 0.83, against 0.04 to 0.77 for
arrays 11 and 12), so its reference is structurally more exposed to
persistence's year-to-year variation. This affects magnitude, not the
shape the paper claims; the full decomposition is in the anonymised
repository accompanying this paper.

\subsection{RQ2: Night Hours Deflate Error, Not Skill}
\label{sec:rq1-night}

Restricting evaluation to daylight hours or retaining all 24 hours
changes reported error substantially and reported skill almost not at
all. If night-time errors are approximately zero, then
\begin{equation}
  \text{RMSE}_{\text{all}} = \text{RMSE}_{\text{daylight}} \times
  \sqrt{N_{\text{daylight}} / N_{\text{all}}}.
  \label{eq:closed-form}
\end{equation}
At six hours on array 11, $N_{\text{daylight}} = 3756$ and
$N_{\text{all}} = 8694$, giving a predicted ratio of 0.657 against an
observed 0.658; agreement is within 0.3 percent at every array and
horizon. Reported normalised error at this site is therefore deflated
by approximately 34 percent by a protocol choice unreported in 17 of
the 27 papers we surveyed (Table~\ref{tab:T1}), and the deflation
factor is a property of latitude and season, not of the forecasting
method. Skill, by contrast, is nearly invariant to the same choice -
for gradient boosting on array 11 it moves from 0.252 to 0.261, 0.527
to 0.528 and 0.652 to 0.652 at one, three and six hours - because the
reference forecast is deflated by the same factor as the model.

\subsection{RQ3: Architecture Contributes Comparatively Little}
\label{sec:rq2-architecture}

Across all five architectures we evaluate - gradient boosting, a
recurrent network, a convolutional-recurrent network, and both
recurrent variants with a gradient-boosted residual correction stage -
the largest effect of any architectural choice we measure on the
held-out test year, including the residual correction stage itself, is
0.086 in skill score. The reference-forecast effect alone at six hours
on array 11, also on the test split (0.701 - 0.232 = 0.469 in skill),
is approximately 5.46 times this largest test-split architectural
effect, measured under an identical protocol. Restricted to the three
base architectures only - excluding the residual correction stage -
the largest test-split pairwise difference is smaller still, 0.028,
making the reference effect approximately 16.6 times larger; this
narrower comparison is a sub-result within the broader one above,
shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:F2}. The largest architectural differences on
test occur at array 17 and six hours - the same array-horizon cell
whose reference behaviour differs most between years, as documented in
Section~\ref{sec:rq1-reference}.

\subsection{Held-Out Test Year}
\label{sec:test-split}

The reference-forecast shape inversion, shown above to hold on the
test split, is not the only direction that does. Night hours remain
deflationary and skill remains nearly unchanged by the same
closed-form argument, which does not depend on which year it is
applied to, and the largest architecture difference remains an order
of magnitude smaller than either protocol effect on the test split as
well as on validation.
