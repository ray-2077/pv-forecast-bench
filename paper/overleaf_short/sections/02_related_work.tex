\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{A Survey of Evaluation Practice}
\label{sec:related-survey}

Aggregate analyses report that hybrid deep learning models
consistently outperform alternatives in this literature
\cite{nguyenmusgens2021drives}; that conclusion aggregates reported
errors, and what follows examines what those errors are measured
against. We coded 27 papers on PV power forecasting, drawn from
searches for hybrid convolutional-recurrent methods published between
2022 and 2026, on eight dimensions of evaluation protocol. Coding was
conservative: a field was recorded as not stated unless the paper
explicitly stated it, and every coded judgement is supported by a
verbatim quotation, recorded in the accompanying repository; 25 of 27
papers were coded this way, and the two coded from summary notes
without a source document are marked as such and excluded from no
count. Table \ref{tab:T8} reports the full coding.

Twenty-six of 27 papers report no skill score against any reference
forecast; seventeen compare only against their own architecture's
components, and three report no comparison model at all. Reported
improvements are therefore improvements over strictly weaker versions
of the same approach. All 27 report point estimates without any
measure of variance across seeds; the seed spread we measure in Section
\ref{sec:rq2} reaches 0.017, of the same order as the largest
architecture difference we find (0.024), so a single-run comparison
cannot separate an architectural effect from retraining noise.
Twenty-two of 27 do not state whether their split preserves temporal
order - three state a chronological split, one uses rolling-origin
evaluation, and one uses k-fold cross-validation, which on a time
series is temporal leakage in the hyperparameter-selection stage
\cite{kapoor2023leakage, hewamalage2023pitfalls}. Seventeen of 27 do
not state whether night hours are excluded, a choice that materially
affects any error metric normalised over all hours (Section
\ref{sec:rq1}).

Three further observations: only one paper makes code available; three
papers describe procedures that constitute leakage under the taxonomy
of Kapoor and Narayanan \cite{kapoor2023leakage} - one decomposes the
full series before splitting [L1.2], and two use target-derived
features as inputs [L2], one of them on this same dataset. We identify
these from the papers' own descriptions only; the 22 papers silent on
split protocol may or may not be affected.

\subsection{Rigorous Practice Exists and Is Not the Norm}
\label{sec:related-rigorous-practice}

The verification of deterministic solar forecasts is treated
extensively elsewhere: Yang et al. \cite{yang2020verification},
writing with more than thirty co-authors, recommend universally
reporting the skill score against the optimal convex combination of
climatology and persistence. One paper in our sample of 27 meets that
standard: Mayer \cite{mayer2022physmlhybrid} evaluates day-ahead
forecasts for fourteen plants against that convex reference, states its
daylight filter as a protocol, and holds out a full year used in no
part of model selection. Its headline hybrid improvement is 5.2 percent
in mean absolute error and 1.0 percent in root-mean-square error over
an optimised physical model chain - an order of magnitude smaller than
improvements typically reported in the sample above, against a
considerably stronger baseline. The standards are published in the same
journals the surveyed papers appear in.

\subsection{Leakage Beyond Solar Forecasting}
\label{sec:related-leakage}

The pattern is not unique to this domain. Kapoor and Narayanan
\cite{kapoor2023leakage} document leakage in at least 294 papers across
17 fields; their civil war prediction case study is directly analogous
to the finding we report - complex models believed to outperform
logistic regression did not, once leakage was corrected. Hewamalage et
al. \cite{hewamalage2023pitfalls} note that random cross-validation
does not preserve temporal order and that rolling-origin evaluation is
itself leakage-susceptible. Nguyen and Musgens
\cite{nguyenmusgens2021drives} find test-set length negatively
correlated with reported accuracy and recommend test sets of at least
one year, a recommendation our design follows.

This paper does not propose an architecture. It fixes a
leakage-controlled protocol and measures how much reported accuracy
moves when the evaluation choices above - shown here to be commonly
unreported - are varied, one at a time, with the model held constant.
